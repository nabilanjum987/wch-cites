## Narration script
This is the Taj Mahal — and this video is the spoken version of a page built to do three things: tell you what is true about it, show you what is live about it, and separate both from the legends.

The facts first, because the internet is careless with them. The Taj Mahal stands in Agra, in the Indian state of Uttar Pradesh, on the southern bank of the Yamuna River. It was commissioned in 1631 by the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan as the tomb of his wife, Mumtaz Mahal, who had died that year. Construction began in 1632. The main structures stood by 1648, and the entire complex — mosque, guest house, great gate, and the charbagh garden — was completed in 1653. The architect recorded by history is Ahmad Lahori. The mausoleum rises seventy-three metres: a white marble dome on a marble plinth, flanked by four minarets, inlaid with semi-precious stones in flowering patterns, at the centre of a seventeen-hectare walled complex. In 1983, UNESCO inscribed it as World Heritage Site number 252 — and our page takes its chronology from UNESCO, its identity from Wikidata, and its visiting rules from the monument's official site, naming each source where you can check it.

Now the living part of the page, because the Taj Mahal is not only history — it is a place you might stand in front of tomorrow. The page carries a live dashboard for the monument itself: Agra's weather right now, the sun's path across the sky, and the golden hours when the reflecting channel turns to glass and photographers line the central axis. On the day we prepared this guide, Agra stood at just over thirty-one degrees, sunrise came at twenty-one minutes past six, and sunset at seventeen forty-two. From the official visiting rule — gates open thirty minutes before sunrise and close thirty minutes before sunset, and the complex is closed every Friday — the page calculates, for the exact moment you open it, whether the Taj Mahal is open now. It also prints the entry fee we verified on the official site: eleven hundred rupees for foreign visitors, dated on the page, because fees change and a guide that hides the date of its facts will eventually mislead you.

Then there are the stories. The black marble twin mausoleum supposedly planned across the river for Shah Jahan himself. The craftsmen whose hands, the legend says, were cut off so the work could never be repeated. Everything you have heard — the dates, the live weather, the opening calculation, the fee, the myths and their verdicts — is on the page below, in writing, where you can check every single line.

Practical notes, all from the page: the complex is at its quietest in the first hour after the gates open, and the light then is the light in the famous photographs. Agra Fort, the red sandstone citadel two kilometres away, is the monument's natural companion visit, and the page's nearby section measures those distances honestly rather than rounding them into marketing.
