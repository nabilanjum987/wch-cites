## Narration script
This is the Southern Ocean — the stormiest water on Earth, the only ocean that circles the entire planet unbroken by land, and the youngest ocean on the map, drawn by agreement rather than discovered by ships.

Hold that last fact a moment, because our page is built around it honestly. The Southern Ocean is generally taken to be the waters south of sixty degrees south latitude, encircling Antarctica — twenty-one million, nine hundred and sixty thousand square kilometres of it, the second-smallest of the five great divisions, larger only than the Arctic. But its northern edge is not a coastline; it is a convention, and a living one: where the cold Antarctic water dives beneath the warmer subantarctic water — the Antarctic Convergence — the oceanographers' boundary breathes north and south with the seasons. Different atlases, and different decades, have drawn the line differently, and some authorities spent years not drawing it at all. The page documents all of that in its identity section, in the open: an ocean whose border is an argument deserves a page that shows the argument, exactly as the golden template showed two depths for the Arabian Sea.

What is not argued is the water itself. Unblocked by continents, the winds of the roaring forties and furious fifties drive the Antarctic Circumpolar Current — the mightiest current on the planet — endlessly eastward around Antarctica, connecting the Pacific, Atlantic, and Indian oceans into one world ocean at this latitude. The page's climatology frame carries that system, labelled as pattern; its live layer reads representative points in the open southern water from the marine feeds and labels them representative, because a single buoy in the fifties is a brave instrument, not an oracle.

Around the fact grid: the krill at the base of the richest cold-water food web on Earth, the research stations on the ice, the treaty that holds a continent for science — each claim sourced, each figure dated at the check, each photograph to be credited to its maker under the registry standard before it ships.

The Southern Ocean is the ocean that tests definitions. Its page below passes the only test this site grades: everything on it can be traced to a source, and everything that is genuinely uncertain is marked uncertain — in full view of the roughest water on Earth.

The page's last sections return to the readers who will never see this ocean — which is nearly everyone, and exactly why it earns its place. The Southern Ocean regulates the planet's heat and feeds, through its upwelling, fisheries on other continents; its storms write the weather diaries of Cape Town, Hobart, and Punta Arenas, the gateway cities whose pages will one day read their skies off this water. A website of cities keeps an ocean page for the same reason a city keeps a lighthouse: not for the street it stands on, but for everything that arrives by sea. The facts are below, sourced and dated; the water, as ever, is doing what it has always done — circling a white continent, waiting for nobody, measured here as honestly as we know how.
