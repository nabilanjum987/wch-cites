## Narration script
This is the Arctic Ocean — the smallest, the shallowest, and the coldest of the world's five oceans, and the one changing fastest while almost nobody watches.

The page begins, as every golden page begins, with the verified frame. The Arctic Ocean spans about fourteen million square kilometres around the North Pole — a Mediterranean of ice ringed by North America, Greenland, Europe, and Siberia. The International Hydrographic Organization recognises it as an ocean in full; some oceanographers call it the Arctic Mediterranean Sea, and it has even been described as, in effect, a great estuary of the Atlantic. Our page prints that debate in its identity section rather than choosing a side in secret — the first lesson the golden template learned, back on the Arabian Sea, was that a guide which hides a naming argument will hide a number next.

Its shallowness is its character: broad continental shelves make much of it a shallow sea under a floating skin of ice, and that ice — its seasonal breathing, its long decline in the satellite record — is the Arctic's live story. The page's live layer is therefore chosen with unusual care: marine readings from the ice-edge waters where real feeds exist, labelled by point and by date, and the conditions of the northern coasts — with the climatology frame carrying the ice pattern, clearly marked as pattern and never passed off as today's measurement. What the page cannot measure, it does not simulate; an Arctic page with invented ice figures would betray the entire standard the template exists to prove.

Around the fact grid sit the ocean's realities as the sources state them: the polar night and the midnight sun at its centre, the research stations and the shipping routes that open and close with the ice, the peoples of its shores whose home this ocean is. Each claim carries its source; each photograph, when the page's images are harvested to the registry standard, will carry its maker and its licence.

The Arctic is where the planet's climate argument becomes a seascape. Its page below refuses to argue beyond the evidence: identity debated in the open, figures sourced, the live layer genuinely live, and the ice — the honest, measured ice — left to speak for itself.

The page closes where the Arctic itself is closing and opening — at the shore. The communities of its coasts, Inuit and Sami, Siberian and Alaskan, do not experience this ocean as a data problem; it is their road in winter and their hunting water in summer, and the page's shore sections treat those coasts as inhabited places, not as the empty rim of a white map. When the site's northern cities publish, they will read their weather off this page's water and lend it their harbours in return. Until then, the ocean page stands on its own evidence: an identity honestly debated, a surface honestly measured, and a skin of ice whose story the sources — not the storytellers — are left to tell.
