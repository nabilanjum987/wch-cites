## Narration script
These are the Pyramids of Giza — the oldest subject on this entire website, older than every other wonder we cover combined, and the only one of the ancient world's seven wonders still standing.

Our page begins where the evidence begins. The largest of the pyramids, the Great Pyramid, was built around 2600 BC as the tomb of Khufu, a pharaoh of Egypt's Fourth Dynasty, and raised over a period of roughly twenty-six years. Beside it stand the pyramids of Khafre and Menkaure, the Sphinx in its quarry hollow, the workers' town, the boat pits, the causeways — a whole necropolis city on the desert edge above the Nile floodplain. UNESCO inscribed the Memphis necropolis, with Giza at its heart, in 1979. Every one of those statements is on the page with its source attached, because for a monument this famous, the difference between knowledge and folklore is the entire job.

For the figures are where folklore breeds. The page's fact grid gives the Great Pyramid's dimensions as the surveys state them, its orientation to true north — accurate to a fraction of a degree, a feat of ancient surveying that needs no embellishment — and its original casing of white limestone, stripped in later centuries, which once made it shine across the valley. What the builders used, how the stones moved, how many hands it took: where scholarship agrees, the page says so; where it argues, the page shows the argument instead of picking a winner in secret.

Then the golden template's living layer, because Giza is not only an excavation — it is a place under a real sky. The page carries Cairo's weather at the plateau, the sunrise that strikes the eastern faces, and the golden hour that photographers cross the world for. Myth versus Fact does its usual honest work: the pyramids were built by paid Egyptian labour, organised and fed by the state — the slave-armies of film are a myth the evidence retired — and the curses, the aliens, and the granite-hard mysteries of the gift shop are labelled with their verdicts where they belong.

The visitor guide stays official and dated: access, the plateau's rules, and the distances measured rather than rounded for marketing. And the photographs credit their makers, as always on this site.

Four and a half thousand years separate you from the builders of this place. The page below is our attempt to cross that distance with nothing but verified facts, a live sky, and respect — check every line; that is what it is for.

The page's closing sections repay the same care. Nearby measures the plateau against Cairo honestly — the city has grown to the desert's edge, and the distance from downtown to the Sphinx is a fact the page states rather than a romance it sells. The photography section names the credited makers of the images and the classic viewpoints they were taken from, including the knoll where the three pyramids align. And the FAQs carry the questions every visitor types at midnight: how long does the plateau take, what does the official entry cover, when does the desert light turn kind. Each answer is short, sourced, and dated where a fee or a rule is involved. Civilisations are remembered by what they built to last. Pages are judged by whether they can be trusted. This one intends to be both.
