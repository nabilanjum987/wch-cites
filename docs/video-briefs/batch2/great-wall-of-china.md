## Facts used
- A series of fortifications across China's historical northern borders, built against nomadic groups of the Eurasian Steppe (Wikipedia).
- First walls date to the 7th century BC; joined under the Qin dynasty; the best-known sections are Ming dynasty (1368–1644).
- UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 1987). Page data row per Round 2 card R2 (QID, coords, licensed images).

## Narration script
This is the Great Wall of China — not one wall, whatever the postcards say, but a system of walls, built and rebuilt across more than two thousand years, and this video follows the page we built to explain it honestly.

Begin with the correction, because it is also the wonder. The first walls rose in the seventh century before Christ, raised by Chinese states against the nomadic peoples of the Eurasian Steppe — and against each other. The Qin dynasty joined the northern sections into a single line. Dynasty followed dynasty: some walls were abandoned to the desert, others rebuilt in stone, and the wall the world pictures today — the dragon-backed ridge lines of brick and rammed earth — is largely the work of the Ming dynasty, between 1368 and 1644. To speak of "the Great Wall" as a single construction project is like speaking of Europe's cathedrals as a single building. It is a civilisation's longest argument with its own frontier.

Our page treats it that way. Its fact grid separates what is known from what is repeated: the dynasties in their order, the materials in their places — tamped earth in the dry west, stone and brick in the mountains near the capital — and the Wall's inscription by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 1987. Its live layer does for the Wall what our golden template does for every wonder: the weather right now at the wall sections visitors actually walk, the sunrise and the golden hour over the ridges, because a monument this size is also a landscape, and its light changes by the hour.

The legends get their own section, as they do on every golden page — Myth versus Fact. No, the Wall cannot be seen from the Moon with the naked eye; astronauts have settled that, and the page says so with its sources attached. No, it is not one continuous unbroken line; the survey maps show branches, doubles, and gaps, and the page shows that too. The true story needs no embroidery: a frontier system maintained, at staggering human cost, across twenty centuries is more astonishing than any myth pasted over it.

Practical notes live where they belong — in the visitor guide: which sections are restored and which are wild, what the official sources say about access, and the honesty that a structure spanning a subcontinent cannot be "visited" in a day, only sampled, well or badly. The photographs carry their makers' names and licences, as every image on this site must.

Everything you have heard — the dynasties, the materials, the live weather, the myths and their verdicts — is set out on the page below, sourced line by line.

Walk the page itself before you close it. The Nearby section measures — really measures — the distances from the famous sections to the cities that serve them, so a traveller can plan a day instead of a hope. The photography section names the viewpoints the credited photographs were taken from, and the FAQs answer the questions visitors actually type: how long is it, in total, across every dynasty — the page gives the survey's answer with its source and its year — and whether one ticket, one day, or one gate exists for a structure that crosses a third of a continent. It does not, and the page's honesty about that is the most useful sentence on it. A wall built by twenty centuries deserves a page built with the same patience: dynasty by dynasty, source by source.
