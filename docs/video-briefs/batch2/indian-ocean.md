## Narration script
This is the Indian Ocean — the third-largest of the world's oceans, the only one named for a country, and the home water of this website's first ocean page.

Begin with the measured frame, as our page does. The Indian Ocean covers seventy million, five hundred and sixty thousand square kilometres — about twenty percent of all the water on Earth's surface. Asia bounds it to the north, Africa to the west, Australia to the east, and to the south it meets the Southern Ocean or Antarctica, depending on the definition in use — a seam the page notes honestly rather than hiding. Its large marginal seas are named in the fact grid: the Andaman Sea, the Bay of Bengal, the Laccadive Sea — and in the northwest, the Arabian Sea.

That last name is why this page stands where it does in our build order. The Arabian Sea was WorldCityHub's golden ocean — the template proof, with its identity matched by description on Wikidata, its two disagreeing depths published side by side, and its live swell measured at sixteen degrees north, sixty-four degrees east. The Indian Ocean page inherits that standard entire: this is the parent sea documented with the same discipline its marginal sea was. A reader moving from one page to the other should feel the same floor underfoot — sources named, figures dated at the check, uncertainty visible.

Above this ocean rules the monsoon, the greatest seasonal wind system on Earth, and the page gives it its labelled climatology frame: the summer winds that carried dhows from Arabia to India and brought the rains three continents plan their farming by; the winter reversal that carried them home. Beside the pattern, the live layer — representative open-water points reporting wave height, direction, and period from the marine feeds, labelled representative; and the coastal weather of the shores our cities stand on, from Karachi at the Arabian Sea's edge to the ocean's other WorldCityHub coasts as they publish.

The Indian Ocean carried the world's oldest long-distance sea trade and still carries a decisive share of its oil. Its page below treats it neither as scenery nor as symbol, but the house way: as a measured, sourced, living body of water — check every line.

End at the shore, where this ocean meets the site's own home water. Karachi stands on the Arabian Sea, and the Arabian Sea answers to this ocean — so the reader who starts at a Karachi beach can climb, page by page, from a city to a sea to an ocean, with the same standard of proof at every step. That ladder is the architecture of the whole website: cities inside countries, seas inside oceans, every level a template, every template filled only with what can be sourced. The monsoon will turn again next season; the figures on the page will be re-checked against their sources; and the ladder will hold, because it was built the slow way — one verified fact at a time.
