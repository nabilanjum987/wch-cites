## Narration script
Welcome to the Arabian Sea — the northwestern arm of the Indian Ocean, and the sea that Pakistan's greatest city calls home.

Geographers define it by its shores and its straits. Arabia bounds it to the northwest, India to the east, and its waters run south toward Socotra and the Horn of Africa. Through the Gulf of Oman it reaches the Persian Gulf; through the Bab-el-Mandeb, the Red Sea. That position made it, for two thousand years, one of the great highways of the world — the sea road between India, Arabia, and East Africa, sailed on the monsoon winds long before steam. Our page identifies it the careful way: matched on its description in the Wikidata knowledge base, record Q58705 — not on the first search result, which happens to be a ship carrying the same name. Identity by description, not by name alone, is a rule this whole site is built on.

Here is an honesty test you can watch us pass. Ask two good sources for the Arabian Sea's maximum depth and you will receive two different answers: the encyclopaedia says five thousand, three hundred and ninety-five metres; the structured data record says five thousand, eight hundred. A careless guide picks whichever it found first and prints it as fact. Our page shows the figure and documents the disagreement openly, in its sources and in its questions section — because a guide that hides its uncertainties will eventually hide its errors, and this sea deserves better bookkeeping.

What the page can show you precisely is the sea's behaviour today. At a representative point in its open water — sixteen degrees north, sixty-four degrees east — a live marine feed reports the height of the waves, the direction they travel from, and the period between them. On the day we prepared this guide, the swell stood at just over a metre, rolling in from the south-southeast with a period of about nine seconds. Along the shore, the same page carries the coastal weather for Karachi, the WorldCityHub city on the sea's northern coast, so you can read the open water and the waterfront together.

Above everything rules the monsoon: the great seasonal reversal of winds that still times the sailing seasons, the fishing calendars, and the rains of three continents. The page explains how the monsoon works — as climatology, clearly labelled as the long pattern — and never confuses the average with the live reading of the day.

This is our golden template for oceans: identity verified, facts sourced, disagreements documented, the live layer genuinely live, and the mythology kept out. Below this video is the page itself, with every figure attached to its source — the standard the whole site is being built to, one page at a time.

For the sailors and the curious alike, the page ends where the sea begins: with the coast. The northern shore runs from the Indus delta past Karachi to the Makran coast and Gwadar, and each of those places reads this same water differently — harbour, fishery, surf, and storm warning. The live point we measure sits far offshore, in the sea's open body, so what it reports is the ocean's own mood before the coast edits it. Read it with the Karachi weather beside it, and you have the sea twice: as it is, and as the city feels it. The other oceans on this site will be built to the same pattern — one sea at a time, each fact earned, each source named, and each uncertainty left visible where a visitor can see it and judge it for themselves — exactly as this sea's page does.
