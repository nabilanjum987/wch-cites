## Narration script
Welcome to Islamabad — the capital of Pakistan, and one of the youngest capital cities in the world.

Islamabad was not inherited; it was designed. In the 1960s, Pakistan decided to build a purpose-made capital, and chose a site at the foot of the Margalla Hills, north of the old garrison city of Rawalpindi. The master plan came from the Greek architect and planner Constantinos Doxiadis: a grid of sectors, wide tree-lined avenues, and green belts threaded through the city — a capital drawn on paper before it was built in stone, at 33.73 degrees north and 73.09 degrees east.

The Margalla Hills are not scenery; they are the city's character. They rise directly behind the sectors, holding hiking trails, viewpoints like Daman-e-Koh, and a wall of green that shapes the weather: when rain comes to Islamabad, it comes off those hills, and the air after it is the cleanest of any big city in Pakistan. Islamabad's residents plan their weekends around the trails the way coastal cities plan around beaches.

Below the hills stands the Faisal Mosque, completed in 1986. It breaks every rule visitors expect: no dome, no arches — instead a vast folded-concrete shell, like a Bedouin tent rendered in marble, designed by the Turkish architect Vedat Dalokay. It remains one of the largest mosques in South Asia and the single most recognisable building in the capital.

This is the seat of Pakistan's government — the presidency, the parliament, the supreme court, the foreign ministries and the diplomatic enclave all sit inside its sectors — and it is deliberately a lower-density city than the country's megacities, which gives its guide a different rhythm.
