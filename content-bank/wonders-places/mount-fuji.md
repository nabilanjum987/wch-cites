## The Story

Mount Fuji is Japan's sacred mountain — a basalt stratovolcano (the packaged material) whose near-perfect cone has been the country's defining image for a thousand years of art, pilgrimage and poetry. The packaged record gives its heritage frame: UNESCO lists it within site 1418 (Fujisan, sacred place and source of artistic inspiration), a cultural landscape inscription that recognises the mountain as an object of worship and art, not only a volcano. Pilgrims climbed it for centuries before tourists did, and the climbing routes still begin at shrines.

## Visitor guide

**Two different visits.** Most visitors *view* Fuji — from the Fuji Five Lakes, from Hakone, or from the Tokaido corridor on a clear day; the mountain is famously shy, and winter mornings give the best odds of an unobstructed cone (the live weather block on the page is the honest forecaster). The other visit is the climb, and it is a season, not a stroll: the official climbing season runs in summer (conventionally early July to early September), when the trails (Yoshida, Subashiri, Gotemba, Fujinomiya) are open with mountain huts; the Yoshida trail now operates with advance-reservation and gate controls set by the authorities. Current season dates, reservation rules and trail conditions are published through the official Mount Fuji climbing information channels — treat those, not any guide, as authoritative, and treat off-season climbing as the dangerous undertaking the authorities say it is.

## What to wear (framework — keyed to the live weather layer)

Read the live conditions block first — for Fuji, altitude is the whole story: the summit is a high mountain in every season, with wind and temperatures far below the plain even in August. For climbers: full mountain kit — layered insulation, waterproof shell, headlamp for the night ascent, sturdy boots, and sun protection for altitude UV. For viewers: ordinary layers, plus patience — the mountain decides.
