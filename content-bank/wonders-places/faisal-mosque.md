## The Story

The Faisal Mosque is Islamabad's signature — a folded-plate concrete shell in white marble (packaged materials), designed by the Turkish architect Vedat Dalokay, completed in 1986 (the packaged inception year, its completion era). Its packaged height is 90 metres to the tips of its four minarets. There is no dome: the prayer hall is a vast tent-form of folded concrete rising against the Margalla Hills, a deliberate break with the classical mosque silhouette that makes it instantly recognisable as the symbol of Pakistan's planned capital. Named for King Faisal of Saudi Arabia, it stands at the northern end of the city's axis, where the avenues meet the hills.

## Visitor guide

**How to visit.** The mosque sits at the foot of the Margalla Hills in Islamabad (see the live Islamabad city pages). It is a working mosque and one of the capital's principal sights at the same time: visitors are welcome outside prayer times, dress modestly, and remove shoes before the courtyard and hall. The courtyard frames the classic photograph — the folded shell against the green hills. Current visiting arrangements are posted at the mosque; as a place of worship there is no museum-style ticket system to describe, and none is invented here.

**Best time.** The hour before sunset, when the marble takes the evening light and the Margallas darken behind it — and, in Islamabad's summer, when the courtyard is bearable (see the live weather block).

## What to wear (framework — keyed to the live weather layer)

Read the live conditions block first, then the mosque rules: modest dress for all — shoulders and knees covered; women cover their hair. Shoes come off; the white marble courtyard is fierce underfoot in summer midday sun and cold on winter mornings — the morning/evening rule serves both comfort and photography.
