## The Story

Machu Picchu is a 15th-century Inca citadel on a mountain ridge 2,430 metres above sea level in Peru's Cusco Region — a royal estate built for the emperor Pachacuti around 1450, occupied for roughly a century, and never found by the Spanish. Some 200 structures of polished dry-stone granite — temples, terraces, houses, storehouses and fountains — step down the ridge between the peaks of Machu Picchu and Huayna Picchu above the Urubamba gorge.

UNESCO inscribed the Historic Sanctuary of Machu Picchu as World Heritage Site 274 in 1983 — 32,500 hectares that are as much a natural sanctuary (orchids, spectacled bears, cloud forest) as an archaeological one. In 2007 the citadel was voted one of the New7Wonders of the World.

The site never "hid" from the people who lived beside it: local farmers knew it, Agustín Lizárraga reached it in 1902, and Hiram Bingham's famous 1911 expedition — led by local farmer Melchor Arteaga — documented and publicised it to the world.

## Visitor guide

**Best time.** Entry is by pre-booked, timed circuit tickets sold through the Peruvian government's official platform — there are no gate sales for the main circuits, and daily numbers are capped to protect the site. The citadel opens at 06:00 and entry slots run through the afternoon; circuits are one-way. Andean afternoons build cloud fast (see the live weather block) — the famous postcard view is a morning prize. The platform publishes the current circuits, caps and prices; fees are deliberately not printed here.

**How to reach.** Almost all visitors arrive via Aguas Calientes (Machupicchu Pueblo): train from Cusco or Ollantaytambo, then the shuttle bus up the switchbacks, or the footpath. Altitude matters: at 2,430 m the citadel is lower than Cusco (3,400 m), so most visitors acclimatise better by sleeping in the Sacred Valley or Aguas Calientes first.

## What to wear (framework — keyed to the live weather layer)

Read the live conditions block first: ridge weather at 2,430 m turns quickly. Layering is the rule in every season — a warm layer for the 06:00 entry, sun protection for midday (high-altitude UV is strong even through cloud), and a waterproof shell for afternoon build-up. Shoes with real grip: the circuits are steep, worn stone, one-way. No umbrellas on the circuits in wind; a packable rain jacket serves better.

## Myth vs Fact

**Myth: Hiram Bingham discovered a lost city nobody knew existed. — FALSE.** Local families farmed the terraces and knew the ruins; Melchor Arteaga led Bingham to the site, tenant families were living at the ruins when he arrived, and Agustín Lizárraga had visited in 1902 and left his name in charcoal. Bingham's achievement was documenting and publicising the citadel. *(Documented visitation history, as recorded in the data row.)*

**Myth: It was the lost Inca city of gold. — FALSE.** Excavation found a royal estate: temples, elite houses, workshops and storehouses, with burials and everyday goods. The "lost city" Bingham hoped for was Vilcabamba — a different site, found later. *(Archaeological record, as recorded in the data row.)*

**Myth: The stones were cut by aliens or unknown technology. — FALSE.** The toolkit is on site: river-cobble hammerstones, bronze tools, and half-finished blocks in the quarry on the ridge itself. Experimental archaeology has reproduced the fitting process with Inca tools — slow, skilled, entirely human work. *(Experimental archaeology and quarry evidence, as recorded in the data row.)*
