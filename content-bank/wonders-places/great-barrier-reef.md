## The Story

The Great Barrier Reef is the world's largest coral reef system and the largest living structure on Earth: some 2,300 km of reef along Queensland's coast, made of roughly 2,900 individual reefs and 900 islands spread over about 344,400 km² of the Coral Sea. It is not one reef but a mosaic — fringing reefs, ribbon reefs, lagoons and coral cays, built by billions of tiny coral polyps over thousands of years.

The system supports extraordinary biodiversity: around 1,500 species of fish, more than 400 hard corals, dugongs, six of the world's seven sea turtles, and seasonal humpback whale nurseries. The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, declared in 1975 and managed by the Great Barrier Reef Marine Park Authority (GBRMPA), zones the whole area; UNESCO inscribed it in 1981 (site 154) — nearly an entire ecosystem in a single listing.

The reef is also the sea country of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, whose connection runs back tens of thousands of years. Its modern story includes mass coral bleaching driven by marine heatwaves — 2016 and 2017 back-to-back, then 2020, 2022, 2024 and a further widespread event reported in 2025: severe damage, monitored reef by reef, but not a death certificate, with recovery between events documented where local pressures are controlled.

## Visitor guide

**How a visit works.** Nobody walks to the Great Barrier Reef — you go by boat, with a permitted tour operator, or not at all. The main departure points are **Cairns and Port Douglas** in the north (the classic outer-reef day trips, roughly 90 minutes to two hours each way to the outer reef pontoons), with further departures from Airlie Beach for the Whitsundays and Townsville. Formats range from snorkelling and introductory diving on outer-reef pontoons, to island day trips (Green Island, Fitzroy Island), glass-bottom boats and semi-submersibles for non-swimmers, and multi-day liveaboards. Every visitor on a commercial tour inside the Marine Park pays an **Environmental Management Charge** set by the Reef Authority — operators either include it in the fare or add it at checkout; current rates are on the GBRMPA site and are deliberately not printed here.

**Best time.** The dry season, roughly May to October, brings the clearest water and calmest seas; humpback whales migrate through from about June to September. The wet season (November to April) is hotter and more humid, with marine stingers present — operators supply stinger suits in season, and swimming follows their guidance.

## What to wear (framework — keyed to the live weather layer)

Read the live conditions block on the page first — for a reef day, the wind and sea state matter more than the air temperature. On the water: reef-safe sunscreen (the standard instruction across operators), a hat that stays on in wind, and a rash vest or the operator's stinger suit in season (roughly November to April). Seasickness is the commonest ruined day: take medication before boarding, not after symptoms start. Footwear stays off coral — never stand on the reef; flotation is provided for snorkellers who want it.

## Myth vs Fact

**Myth: The reef is a single organism. — FALSE.** It is an ecosystem of thousands of reefs and billions of individual coral colonies of hundreds of species — vast and connected, but not one creature. *(Myth: "largest living thing" shorthand; correction: Wikipedia — "Great Barrier Reef", as recorded in the data row.)*

**Myth: The reef is dead. — FALSE.** Mass bleachings have killed large areas of coral, especially in the north in 2016–17, and the threat is real and continuing. But monitoring shows substantial living reef and documented recovery in many sectors — "damaged in places, alive in others" is the scientific summary. *(GBRMPA monitoring, as recorded in the data row.)*

**Myth: You can see it from the Moon. — FALSE.** Like the Grand Canyon claim, this fails on distance: the reef is visible from low Earth orbit, but at lunar distance nothing of its scale resolves to the naked eye. *(Wikipedia — "Great Barrier Reef".)*
