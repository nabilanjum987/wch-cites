## The Story

The Basílica de la Sagrada Família is Antoni Gaudí's unfinished masterpiece in Barcelona's Eixample district — under construction since the first stone of 19 March 1882 and, by design, a building its architect knew he would never see finished. Gaudí took over in 1883, aged 31, and devoted the last 43 years of his life to it, living on site at the end; he is buried in its crypt.

Gaudí's church replaces Gothic structure with geometry drawn from nature: columns that branch like trees, hyperboloid vaults that pour light down the nave, and three grand façades — Nativity (the only one he saw substantially complete), Passion (sculpted by Josep Maria Subirachs) and the still-rising Glory. Pope Benedict XVI consecrated it as a basilica in 2010, with the interior complete enough for worship. The church is funded entirely by donations and visitor income — no church or state money.

When its central Tower of Jesus Christ reaches the planned 172.5 metres — deliberately just under Montjuïc hill, as Gaudí insisted man's work should not exceed God's — the Sagrada Família will be the tallest church building in the world. UNESCO inscribed the crypt and Nativity façade within the Works of Antoni Gaudí (site 320) in 1984, extended in 2005.

## Visitor guide

**Best time and booking.** Tickets are **online-only, nominative and timed**, sold through the Basilica's official site (sagradafamilia.org) — there is no general walk-up ticket office. Choose at booking: basilica entry, entry with guided tour, or entry with one tower (one tower per ticket — there is no both-towers ticket; tower access is weather-dependent). Peak-season slots sell out well ahead. Arrive 15–20 minutes before your slot for the security check, and bring the QR ticket on your phone.

**Good to know.** Personal photography is allowed inside (no flash, tripods or commercial shoots). Sunday's international Mass is a worship service, not a sightseeing route. Prices change and are deliberately not printed here — the official site carries the current figures.

## What to wear (framework — keyed to the live weather layer)

Read the live conditions block on the page first, then the dress rules for an active Catholic church: shoulders covered in the chapel areas, no swimwear or see-through clothing, and trousers or skirts reaching at least mid-thigh; hats come off inside the nave and museum. In Barcelona's summer, meet the code with light, breathable coverage rather than bare shoulders — and carry a layer for the tower ascent, which is exposed and windy.

## Myth vs Fact

**Myth: It will be finished in 2026. — FALSE (as a whole-building claim).** 2026 was a widely repeated target tied to the Gaudí centenary, and the central tower's completion is its centrepiece — but the whole church, including the Glory façade and its staircase, is a longer programme. "Finished" headlines should be read as tower headlines. *(Myth: press shorthand; correction: Wikipedia — "Sagrada Família", as recorded in the data row.)*

**Myth: Gaudí designed every part you see. — FALSE.** Gaudí left models and rules, not a complete design: the Passion façade sculpture is Subirachs's (1987–2009), and later towers follow Gaudí's geometry executed by successor architects. *(Wikipedia — "Sagrada Família".)*

**Myth: It is Barcelona's cathedral. — FALSE.** Barcelona's cathedral is the Gothic Cathedral of the Holy Cross in the old town. The Sagrada Família is a basilica and parish church — an expiatory temple funded by donations. *(Wikipedia — "Sagrada Família".)*
