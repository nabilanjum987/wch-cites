# Interbank vs Open Market Rates — Explained

*Evergreen explainer for the rates hub. It explains the two USD/PKR prices Pakistanis meet every day. It quotes one dated sample, labelled as such; all other numbers in it are structural, not rates.*

## Two markets, two prices

Pakistan's rupee has two exchange rates on any given day, and the difference between them confuses everyone the first time they meet it.

The **interbank rate** is the wholesale price of dollars. It is the rate at which banks trade foreign currency with each other, in large amounts, during banking hours. The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) publishes the day's interbank rates — the mark-to-market weighted averages at which the banks actually dealt. This is the rate that prices imports and exports, settles letters of credit, services official debt, and converts the remittances that arrive through banking channels. When news reports say "the rupee closed at X against the dollar," they mean the interbank rate.

The **open market rate** is the retail price. It is what the exchange companies — the licensed currency dealers regulated by the SBP — quote over the counter to a person buying or selling notes: a traveller buying dollars before a flight, a student paying fees, a family converting cash sent home. Open market rates are set by the dealers' own supply and demand on top of the interbank anchor, and they differ slightly from shop to shop and city to city.

## Why the two rates differ

The open market almost always prices the dollar a little higher than the interbank rate — the dealer's margin for holding cash, paying rent and taking the risk of the physical note trade. In calm times the gap is small, a fraction of a percent to about a percent. The gap is also a diagnostic: when it widens sharply, it signals that dollars are scarce or distrusted in the cash economy — and historically, a wide gap is what pulls transactions out of the banking system into informal channels. Narrowing the gap is one of the stated aims of SBP policy, precisely because the formal channels are the ones that build the country's reserves and records.

The informal alternative has a name — hundi/hawala — and a legal status: it is illegal under Pakistan's Foreign Exchange Regulation Act, offers the sender no receipt the courts will honour, and is the channel anti-money-laundering enforcement exists to close. The open market's licensed dealers are the legal cash channel.

## Which rate applies to you?

- **Remittance arriving in a bank account or mobile wallet:** converted at the bank's applied rate, which is anchored to the interbank market. (See the remittance guide in this bank.)
- **Cash dollars in hand, sold to a dealer:** the open market rate of that dealer, that day.
- **Buying dollars for travel:** the open market rate, with the dealer's sell price — always the less favourable side of the spread.
- **Trade, tuition paid through a bank, official transactions:** the interbank rate, through your bank.
- **Gold:** the sarafa board converts the international price using the interbank parity (see "How Pakistan gold pricing works" in this bank) — which is why gold and the interbank rate move together in the news.

## A dated sample — how to read one day's pair

> **Sample, 22 October 2026 (labelled, not a standing fact):** public rate boards that day showed interbank USD/PKR at **277.00** and the open market at about **279.77** — a gap of roughly Rs 2.77, or about 1%. Read it as an illustration of a normal, calm spread: the wholesale price and the retail price of the same dollar, one working day, about one percent apart.

Tomorrow's figures will differ; the structure will not. The number to check is always the day's pair — the interbank close published by the SBP, and the open-market quote of the dealer in front of you.
